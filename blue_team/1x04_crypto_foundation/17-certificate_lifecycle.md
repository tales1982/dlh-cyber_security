## Auto-Renewal Strategy

**Recommendation: ACME/Let's Encrypt (or an ACME-compatible commercial CA) for the patient portal specifically**, for reasons directly grounded in this project's own findings rather than a generic best-practice statement:

- **The root cause of the current emergency was a manual renewal process**, not a certificate type choice Finding 013's 18-days-to-expiration crisis happened *because* renewal depended on a human remembering to act, and a 1-year manual-CA certificate only reduces how often that human has to remember, it does not remove the dependency on them remembering at all.
- **800 daily patient connections is a volume that justifies automation, not manual process, on its own merits** a portal serving that much real patient traffic cannot tolerate the downtime risk of a missed manual renewal window; automating the entire renewal via ACME removes the human-memory failure point structurally, the same way T10's playbook already emphasized monitoring/renewal automation as the fix for this exact near-miss.
- **The shorter 90-day ACME lifetime is a feature here, not a drawback** Task 8/9 already found that both real-world examples inspected (Let's Encrypt and GitHub's commercial DV certificate) use ~90-day lifetimes today; shorter validity limits the exposure window if a key is ever compromised, and since renewal is fully automated, the shorter cycle costs nothing in operational effort while directly reducing risk.
- **Cost is also a relevant factor for MedDefense specifically** 1x03's $120,000 total security budget is under constant competing pressure (SIEM, MFA, segmentation); a free ACME certificate removes a recurring cost line entirely for a control that delivers no additional security benefit over the same organization paying for a manual commercial certificate.

**For internal-only certificates (EHR internal, future VPN certs)**, a MedDefense-operated **internal CA** (not a public ACME CA, since these hostnames are not internet-routable) is more appropriate internal PKI lets MedDefense control the validity period and revocation process without depending on any external party, appropriate for infrastructure only MedDefense's own systems ever need to trust.

## Certificate Policy

1. **All public-facing services must use certificates from a CA trusted by mainstream browsers (Let's Encrypt or an equivalent ACME-compatible/commercial public CA); all internal-only services must use certificates issued by MedDefense's internal CA. Self-signed certificates are prohibited in production, without exception.** directly closes the exact gap Task 8, Part 2 demonstrated (a browser cannot distinguish a benign self-signed certificate from an attacker's) and prevents the ad-hoc, undocumented certificate issuance that produced today's "no inventory" problem.
2. **No certificate may be issued, renewed, or installed using a key algorithm weaker than RSA-2048 or ECC P-256, per the Algorithm Reference Table (T6).** ties every future certificate decision back to the same standard already applied throughout this project (T8, T10), preventing a well-intentioned but technically weak certificate from being issued in the future.
3. **Every certificate tracked in the Certificate Inventory must have a named individual owner (not a team or department) responsible for its renewal, recorded before the certificate is issued.** directly targets the root cause of Finding 013's near-miss: a certificate with no clearly accountable owner is a certificate nobody notices expiring until it already has.
4. **Public-facing certificates must not exceed 90 days of validity; internal CA-issued certificates must not exceed 1 year.** codifies the auto-renewal strategy above as policy rather than a one-time recommendation, ensuring the next IT hire or contractor follows the same standard without needing to rediscover the reasoning independently.
5. **Any certificate private key exposure (e.g., committed to a Git repository, as in 1x03's MCQ T25 scenario) must trigger immediate revocation and replacement per the procedure documented in T9, Part 3, reported to James Chen within 24 hours regardless of whether exploitation is confirmed.** makes the incident-response sequence already designed in this project (T9) an enforceable policy obligation rather than a document that only gets read after the fact.
